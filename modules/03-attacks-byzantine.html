Network attacks and Byzantine agreement

Nodes do not always see the same messages at the same time. Some participants may create many identities, isolate a peer, or deliberately send conflicting information. This module names those risks and uses the generals story to explain why agreement is difficult.

1. Think in terms of a threat model

A network threat model states what an attacker can control and what guarantees we want to preserve. A crash fault is a participant that stops working. A Byzantine fault is more general: a participant may behave arbitrarily, send different messages to different peers, or lie about what it received.

Do not confuse normal disagreement caused by network delay with deliberate equivocation. A system may see two valid block proposals before those blocks propagate; that can cause a temporary fork even when every miner follows the rules.

2. Sybil and eclipse attacks

AttackWhat the attacker changesWhy it matters
SybilOne actor presents many apparent identities or nodes.A system that treats identities as independent may give one actor disproportionate influence or peer-selection power.
EclipseThe attacker controls or heavily influences the connections of one target node.The isolated node may see a filtered or misleading view of transactions and blocks.

They are related because Sybil identities can help an attacker obtain connections, but they describe different parts of the attack: many false identities versus isolating a victim’s network view. Proof of work ties influence to computational effort; permissioned systems may use known identities and admission rules. Neither choice removes every networking or governance risk.

3. Forks and the main chain

Suppose two miners find different valid blocks that extend the same parent at nearly the same time. Some nodes hear about the first block before the second; other nodes see the reverse order. Each branch can be valid at first, so the network temporarily has two tips.

Bitcoin discussions often call this the longest-chain rule. More precisely, nodes follow the valid chain with the greatest cumulative proof of work. More confirmations on a transaction’s block usually make a competing history more expensive to build.

Not the same as “the longest list of blocks”Two branches can have different work per block in general. The security comparison is about accumulated work, while every block must still pass validation rules.

4. The Byzantine Generals Problem

The story describes several generals who surround a city. They must choose a common plan—such as attack or retreat—by sending messages. Some generals may be traitors. A traitor can lie, change a message, or tell different people different things.

In a computer system, generals stand for nodes; messages stand for network communication; the plan stands for a decision such as which value or history to accept. The important lesson is not the medieval setting. It is that correct participants need a rule that works even when some participants are malicious.

GoalPlain-language meaning
AgreementAll loyal participants decide on the same value.
ValidityIf the commander is loyal, loyal participants follow the commander’s value.

The precise result depends on assumptions: how many participants may be traitors, who knows the sender, whether messages arrive, whether signatures are available, and what “agreement” requires. For the exam’s three-generals example, focus on the oral-message setting used in class: a sender’s identity may be known, but a traitor can send inconsistent contents.

5. Why three generals and one traitor are not enough

Take one commander and two lieutenants. A loyal commander should be obeyed. But if the commander is traitorous, it can send “Attack” to one lieutenant and “Retreat” to the other. A traitorous lieutenant can also misreport what the commander said.

  1. Indistinguishable views: a loyal lieutenant may receive the same local messages in two different worlds—one where the commander is loyal and a lieutenant lies, and another where the commander equivocates.
  2. No local test reveals the truth: the lieutenant cannot infer from those messages alone which participant is the traitor.
  3. Conflicting requirements: a decision rule that obeys a loyal commander in one world can leave loyal lieutenants disagreeing in the other.

So, under the oral-message assumptions, three participants cannot guarantee both agreement among loyal participants and validity when one may be traitorous. The result is about the stated model, not a claim that every real system with three computers must fail.

Two-generals problem versus Byzantine generals

The two-generals problem focuses on unreliable communication and the difficulty of common knowledge: even if a message and acknowledgment are sent, the sender may not know whether the acknowledgment arrived. The Byzantine generals problem adds participants who may behave maliciously and send conflicting information. Keep the two problems distinct when answering.

Quick check

How is an eclipse attack different from a Sybil attack?

A Sybil attacker creates many apparent identities. An eclipse attack isolates or controls one victim’s peer connections and therefore its view of the network.

Why can two valid Bitcoin blocks temporarily coexist?

They may be mined on the same parent before information about one reaches every node. Nodes later converge using the valid branch with greatest cumulative work.

What are agreement and validity?

Agreement means loyal participants choose the same value. Validity means loyal participants follow a loyal commander’s value.

What is the core intuition behind the three-generals impossibility example?

Different worlds with different traitors can produce the same local messages for a loyal participant. The participant cannot tell which world it is in, so a single decision rule cannot satisfy both agreement and validity in every case.

Exam-ready summary

  • State what an attacker controls in Sybil and eclipse attacks.
  • Explain how propagation delay can create a temporary fork without a protocol violation.
  • Use “greatest cumulative proof of work” to explain Bitcoin chain selection.
  • Define agreement and validity separately.
  • Explain the three-generals impossibility intuition through indistinguishable views.
  • State the message-model assumptions before stating a Byzantine result.
